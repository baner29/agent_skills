# Orwell's judgment: when the rules bend

The banned list catches known words. The six rules catch known habits. This file covers the ground between them: the cases where applying a rule blindly would damage the sentence, and where no rule names the fault at all. Load it when a rule and a sentence pull apart.

## The diagnosis underneath the rules

Orwell's charge against bad prose was not ugliness. It was insincerity. "The great enemy of clear language is insincerity." A writer who lets ready-made phrases crowd in has stopped choosing words and started assembling chunks, and the chunks think for him. Machine text is that habit at industrial scale: the highest-probability phrase, laid end to end, saying less than it appears to say.

So the editor's first question of any suspect sentence is not "which rule does this break?" It is "did anyone mean this, word by word?" A sentence the writer chose, however rough, outranks a smooth sentence nobody chose.

## Rule 6 governs

"Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous." Orwell wrote the exception into the rules themselves, and his own practice proves he meant it. In *Politics and the English Language*, the essay that says never use the passive where you can use the active, roughly 20 to 26 percent of the verb constructions are passive. The journalistic norm is about 13 percent. He kept a passive whenever the topic, the rhythm, or plain sense asked for one, and he cut it when it was evasion. The rule was a tool. The judgment was the craft.

## Calibration, rule by rule

- **The passive (Rule 4).** Prefer the active. Keep the passive when the thing acted on is the sentence's real subject, when the actor is unknown or beside the point, or when the active version twists the sentence out of shape. "The bridge was completed in 1890" loses nothing to "Workers completed the bridge in 1890" and gains a fact the original never stated. Leave it.
- **Long words (Rule 2).** A long word that is the exact term stays. *Photosynthesis*, *jurisdiction*, and *latency* are not jargon; they are the names of things. The rule targets the long word chosen to impress, where a short one carries the same meaning.
- **Metaphors (Rule 1).** A metaphor you are used to seeing in print is dead: it pictures nothing. In an edit, cut it or flatten it to the plain claim underneath. Do not replace it with a fresher metaphor of your own. Inventing images is the writer's privilege, and the last-mile rule forbids it. When the claim underneath a dead metaphor cannot be recovered from the text, flag it for the user instead of guessing.
- **Cutting (Rule 3).** Cut the word that carries nothing. Keep the word that carries tone. A writer's "rather" or "quite" may be doing quiet work; a machine's "very" and "really" almost never are. When in doubt, the tie goes to the writer.
- **Foreign and jargon words (Rule 5).** An everyday English equivalent must carry the *same* meaning, not a nearby one. Where the plain word blurs a distinction the text depends on, the technical word stays.

## Worked judgments

**A passive that stays.** Orwell, in *Shooting an Elephant*: "I was only an absurd puppet pushed to and fro by the will of those yellow faces behind." The passive is the meaning. The narrator is acted upon; forcing the sentence active would move the helplessness out of it. An editor who "fixed" this would have broken the finest sentence in the essay.

**A plain swap that works.** "Our team will leverage cutting-edge solutions to streamline your workflow and unlock valuable insights" becomes "Our team will use modern tools to simplify your workflow and find valuable insights." Every banned word had an everyday equal, and the claim survived word for word. This is the easy case, and most machine sentences are this case.

**Scaffolding, cut whole.** "It is important to note that the meeting has been moved to Friday" becomes "The meeting has moved to Friday." The first five words announce; the rest states. Announcements are machine habit. Statements are prose.

**A dead metaphor, flagged not fixed.** "A tapestry of sound that seemed to transcend the physical world" pictures nothing and claims nothing recoverable: rich sound? loud sound? beautiful sound? The text does not say. The editor's move is to flag it for the writer, not to invent the sense it failed to make.

## Rhythm is the writer's

Orwell's own prose alternates long, built-up sentences with blunt five-word verdicts, and that contrast is part of why it reads as human. In an edit, protect the contrast where the writer has it. Do not manufacture it where they do not: splitting a writer's long sentence into three short ones to fake a pulse is a structural change wearing an editor's clothes. Rhythm faults earn an edit only when they are machine habits, such as a run of sentences all opening the same way, and then the fix is the smallest one that breaks the run.

## The final test

Before returning any edit, ask Orwell's sixth question of the result: have I said anything that is avoidably ugly? A correct edit that reads worse than the fault it cured has failed. Undo it, and let the sentence stand.
